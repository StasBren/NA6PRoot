# NA60+ MWPC cathode signal simulation — group-meeting oral draft
*Working speaker text, 20 November 2026. Approximately 6–8 minutes. No VMM/electronics.*

Good morning. I would like to give an update on our simulation of induced cathode signals in the NA60+ muon chambers. Our long-term objective is to turn a simulated muon crossing into realistic induced signals on individual readout strips, while avoiding the cost of running a full microscopic gas avalanche for every event.

I will distinguish the physics we have already simulated from the readout geometry we are only starting to develop.

We begin with genuine Geant4 muon crossings. TrackHeed generates the primary ionization electrons along the track in an Ar/CO2 gas mixture. Garfield++ then transports those electrons microscopically in the MWPC electric field and simulates avalanche multiplication close to the anode wires. During the simulation we record the number of created electron–ion pairs, the positions and times of positive-ion births, and the electrons eventually collected at the anodes.

For the first controlled study, we have used an ideal numerical chamber: nine 30-micrometre anode wires with 3 mm pitch, symmetric cathodes 3 mm from the anodes, and 2100 V. This is a *reference model*, not a claim that it represents the finished chamber design. We also use a provisional positive-ion mobility, so the calculated slow-ion times should not be interpreted as calibrated detector predictions.

Our first detailed benchmark is one genuine simulated J/psi-source muon crossing. TrackHeed produced seven primary electrons, and Garfield++ generated 554,943 avalanche ions and 555,139 collected electrons. In the direct response calculation, we use a weighted sample of 3,072 ion-birth positions, rather than individually drifting all half a million ions. For each sampled positive ion, Garfield++ DriftLineRKF numerically integrates its motion in the electric field. The induced current is obtained using the Shockley–Ramo weighting-field method. We calculate the electron-induced contribution separately from the microscopic electron trajectories, including the secondary electrons created in the avalanche.

This gives us two ideal whole-cathode current waveforms, with separate electron and positive-ion contributions. For the benchmark event, the integrated ion contributions over 100 microseconds are approximately +87.61 fC on the upper cathode and -0.743 fC on the lower. Including the microscopic electron contributions, the totals are approximately +88.70 fC and +0.271 fC. These are induced-charge integrals on unsegmented ideal cathodes, *not* collected strip charges, measured voltages or electronics pulses.

The direct ion transport is relatively expensive. To accelerate it, we built a small library of 256 precomputed single-ion responses. Each entry is the upper and lower induced-current waveform from one positive ion born at a particular position near the wires. For a new ion starting position, our fast model finds four nearby templates and interpolates between their waveforms. We shift the predicted waveform by the ion's actual birth time, multiply by its statistical sampling weight and sum over the sampled ions. For the perfectly symmetric two-plane model we can reflect a RESPONSE template between hemispheres and exchange the upper and lower signals; we do not reflect or artificially copy the physical avalanche ion population.

Our first 100 ns-binned implementation reproduced the final integrated charge quite closely, but underestimated the induced ion charge during the first 100 ns by about 20% on the upper plane and 22% on the lower. We isolated this discrepancy by keeping the exact same ion population and the same 256 training positions, while recalculating the ion-response waveforms using 10 ns bins. The original direct RKF waveform was unchanged when the fine result was rebinned to 100 ns. However, the new fast model's first-100-ns charge errors decreased to +0.206% and +0.177% respectively. The remaining full waveform errors at native 10 ns resolution are about 2.94% and 3.45% in relative L2 norm. Therefore the original problem was primarily in how coarse response templates were time-shifted, not in a failure of the ion trajectory calculation. We should not describe 0.2% as a universal waveform-accuracy claim.

In parallel, we have generated a considerably larger microscopic input dataset. In our audited snapshot of 572 selected muon slots, 571 passed the ROOT and uncapped-physics checks. These muons come from interleaved J/psi, phi and omega source samples across all six stations. They generated approximately 1.053 billion microscopic avalanche-ion births, represented by 6.23 million stored weighted ion positions. The mean effective collected-electron gain per original Heed electron is around 36,000, with important event-to-event fluctuations. These 571 events are microscopic avalanche archives: they are not yet 571 calculated cathode waveforms. One event that failed its audit is explicitly excluded rather than silently treated as a zero response.

The next response validation is to test the frozen 256-template model on three independent, already-audited muons from different stations and production samples. We will compare 10 ns direct RKF and fast ion waveforms using the same sampled ions in each event. This will tell us whether the template library generalizes beyond the first training muon before we consider increasing its size.

Our next detector-geometry target is the simpler two-sided stereo-strip design currently under discussion. Its proposed MuTr readout uses approximately 1.7 mm-wide strips, with the width defined along the anode-wire direction; the strips are inclined in opposite directions on the two cathodes, with tan(alpha)=0.1. Symmetric 3+3 mm cathode gaps are a candidate, while 2.5+2.5 mm is also discussed. These are design assumptions to be checked, not final hardware parameters. We are first validating the U/V strip coordinates and an analytic, wire-free boundary-field control. This control must not be confused with the real strip weighting fields: an accurate field calculation still needs the grounded anode wires and ultimately the PCB, conductor gaps and finite geometry. That requires a three-dimensional wire-aware electrostatic solution and convergence tests before we calculate physical strip currents.

To summarize, we now have microscopic avalanche statistics for hundreds of muons, one full-muon demonstration of separate electron and ion currents on both ideal cathodes, and a fast ion-response calculation whose large initial timing artifact has been resolved at 10 ns. Our immediate priorities are independent-muon validation and the physically relevant stereo-strip weighting fields. We are deliberately not moving to front-end electronics yet. The outstanding limitations—ion mobility, ion-reservoir sampling, idealized geometry, possible collective effects and the original Heed positive ions—remain explicit in our approximation register.
